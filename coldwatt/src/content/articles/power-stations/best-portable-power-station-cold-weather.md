## How we chose

We haven't bench-tested these units — see [how we research](/how-we-research/). Instead, we compared them on what decides whether a power station helps you in a Canadian winter:

1. **Output** — can it start and run a furnace blower, a fridge, or both?
2. **Capacity** — how many hours will it last with realistic loads?
3. **Published cold limits** — does the manufacturer tell you where it stops working and charging?
4. **Chemistry** — LiFePO4 batteries handle far more charge cycles than older lithium types.
5. **UPS switchover** — does it keep gear running when the grid drops?

Every spec comes from the manufacturer's own spec sheet, linked at the bottom of this page.

## The honest truth about "cold-weather" power stations

No consumer power station we've found is built to sit outside through a prairie January. Every model we've checked that publishes a charging limit stops charging at 0°C, and the lowest published operating temperature we've found is −20°C (the Anker SOLIX C1000 Gen 2). The best "cold-weather power station" is really **a good power station kept in a warm place**. Choose on output and capacity for your needs, then plan where it will live.
