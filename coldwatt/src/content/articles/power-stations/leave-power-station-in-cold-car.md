**Short answer:** you can usually *store* a power station in a cold vehicle, but don't expect to *use* or *charge* it there in a prairie winter. Units that publish their limits often stop running somewhere below freezing — Jackery rates the Explorer 1000 v2 to −10°C — and most lithium batteries won't accept a charge below 0°C. A truck cab on a January night in Alberta can blow past both.

## Three different limits

Every power station has three temperature ranges, and they're not the same:

1. **Storage** — how cold or hot it can sit, switched off, without harm. Usually the widest range. Look for it in the user manual.
2. **Operating (discharging)** — the range in which it will power your devices. Below the minimum, it shuts itself off.
3. **Charging** — the range in which it will refill. This is the narrowest, and it almost always starts at or near **0°C**.

As an example, Jackery publishes an operating range of **−10°C to 45°C** and a charging range of **0°C to 45°C** for the Explorer 1000 v2. Many brands don't publish these numbers on their product pages at all — we track the ones that do in our [cold-weather spec database](/tools/cold-weather-power-station-specs/).

## What a cold vehicle actually does to it

Once the engine's off, a parked car or truck cools to roughly the outdoor temperature within hours. So a power station left in the cab overnight will be about as cold as the night was.

- **It may not turn on in the morning** if it's below its operating minimum. Warm it up first.
- **It will run for less time** even above the minimum, because cold batteries deliver less energy. Our [runtime calculator](/tools/winter-runtime-calculator/) lets you compare indoor and cold-vehicle runtimes.
- **It won't charge** from the 12V outlet, solar panels or a wall plug until the battery warms above its charging minimum. That's deliberate — [charging frozen lithium cells damages them](/power-stations/can-you-charge-lifepo4-below-freezing/).

## How to keep one usable on winter trips

- **Bring it inside at night** — into the house, cabin, heated tent or motel room.
- **Charge while you drive,** once the cab has warmed up, rather than first thing on a frozen morning.
- **Insulate it.** An insulated cooler or bag slows cooling on shorter stops. Take it out of the insulation while it's running so the vents stay clear.
- **Store it partly charged** if it'll sit for a while, as many manufacturers recommend — check your manual.
- **Mind the summer too.** A car in the sun gets far hotter than the air outside, and batteries have upper limits as well. Keep it off the dashboard and out of direct sun.

## The bottom line

A power station can ride along in your vehicle all winter, but treat the cab like a freezer: fine for storage, bad for using or charging. If you need power in a cold vehicle or truck camper, choose a model that publishes a low operating temperature, or look at batteries with built-in heaters designed for low-temperature charging.
