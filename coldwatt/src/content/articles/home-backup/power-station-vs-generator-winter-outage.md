When the power goes out in January, you have two realistic ways to keep essentials running: a **battery power station** or a **fuel generator**. They solve the problem in very different ways, and the right choice mostly comes down to one question: **how long are your outages?**

## The quick comparison

| | Power station (battery) | Generator (gas, propane) |
|---|---|---|
| **Where it runs** | Indoors | Outdoors only, 6 m+ from the house |
| **Noise** | Silent or near-silent | Loud |
| **Exhaust / CO risk** | None | Serious — needs CO alarms and distance |
| **How long it lasts** | Until the battery runs out (hours) | As long as you have fuel (days) |
| **Switching on** | Instant; some switch over automatically | Must be started, outside, in the cold |
| **Recharging / refuelling** | Needs grid power, solar or a generator | Needs stored fuel |
| **Maintenance** | Very little | Oil changes, fuel stabilizer, test runs |
| **Cold-weather catch** | Won't charge below about 0°C; loses capacity when cold | Can be hard to start in deep cold |

## Safety comes first: carbon monoxide

This is the biggest difference, and it matters more in winter, when houses are sealed up and people are tempted to run a generator somewhere sheltered.

Health Canada is clear: **never use a fuel-burning generator indoors or in your garage or shed**. Run it at least **6 metres (20 feet)** from buildings, with the exhaust pointed away from windows and doors, and close the windows closest to or downwind of it. Even a generator with an automatic CO shutoff doesn't replace carbon monoxide alarms in your home.

A battery power station has none of these risks. It makes no exhaust, so it can sit right next to the fridge or the furnace.

## When a power station makes more sense

- **Most of your outages last hours, not days.** A 1,000 Wh unit can keep a fridge going overnight or a furnace blower running for a few hours. Work out your own numbers with the [winter runtime calculator](/tools/winter-runtime-calculator/).
- **You need power the instant the grid drops.** Many power stations switch over in milliseconds, keeping a modem, CPAP or furnace control board running without a hiccup.
- **You live in an apartment or condo**, where a generator isn't an option.
- **You want it simple.** No fuel to store, no pull-cord at −30°C, no noise complaints.

The limit is energy. When the battery's empty, it needs grid power, sunshine, or a generator to refill it — and most lithium batteries won't accept a charge below freezing. [Here's why](/power-stations/can-you-charge-lifepo4-below-freezing/).

## When a generator makes more sense

- **Your area gets multi-day outages**, especially rural properties at the end of a long line.
- **You need to run big loads for a long time**, like a well pump plus a furnace for days.
- **You're comfortable with the upkeep**: storing fuel safely, running it regularly, and setting it up outside in winter conditions.

## The combination most people end up with

For longer winter outages, the two work well together. Run the generator **outside, during the day**, to power the house and recharge the power station. At night, shut the generator off and let the **silent power station** carry the fridge, furnace fan and devices until morning. You burn less fuel, sleep without the noise, and never have a generator running unattended in the dark.

## Connecting either one to your furnace

Most furnaces are hard-wired, so plugging them into anything requires a **transfer switch or inlet installed by a licensed electrician**. Never backfeed your house through a wall outlet. Our guide to [running a furnace on a power station](/home-backup/power-station-furnace-power-outage/) explains the options.

## The bottom line

If your outages are measured in hours, a power station is the safer, simpler choice. If they're measured in days, you'll want fuel in the mix — ideally alongside a power station for quiet, safe overnight power.
