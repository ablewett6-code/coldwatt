Solar at a summer cabin is easy. Solar at a cabin you use in January is a different project. Here's how to plan a system that still works when the days are short and the thermometer reads −30°C.

## Size for December, not June

The biggest mistake in cold-climate solar is sizing from summer numbers. In central Alberta and much of Canada, a panel can produce a small fraction of its June output in December, because of:

- **Short days** — often under eight hours of daylight in late December.
- **Low sun angle** — the sun stays low, so light passes through more atmosphere.
- **Snow** — a panel covered in snow produces almost nothing until it's cleared or slides off.

The cold itself is not the problem. Solar panels are slightly *more* efficient when cold. It's the lack of sunlight that hurts.

**Get real numbers for your site.** NREL's free [PVWatts calculator](https://pvwatts.nrel.gov/) estimates monthly production for any location, panel size and tilt. Enter your cabin's location and compare December against June — it's usually eye-opening.

## Tilt steep and keep panels clear

- **Steeper is better in winter.** A steep tilt catches more low-angle sun and helps snow slide off on its own.
- **Make them reachable.** Ground-mounted panels you can brush off beat roof panels you can't.
- **Watch for shade.** Winter shadows are long. A tree that's harmless in July can shade your panels all day in December.

## Protect the battery from the cold

This is where cabin systems usually fail. Most lithium batteries **can't be charged below 0°C**, so a battery in a frozen cabin can't store the solar energy your panels produce. Options:

1. **Keep the battery in the warmest spot** and insulate it — though an unheated cabin will still freeze eventually.
2. **Use a battery with a built-in heater** designed for low-temperature charging.
3. **Take the power station home between visits** and bring it back charged.

Read more in [can you charge a LiFePO4 battery below freezing?](/power-stations/can-you-charge-lifepo4-below-freezing/)

## Estimate what you need

List what you'll run at the cabin — lights, a water pump, a fan for a wood stove or furnace, device charging — and estimate daily watt-hours. The [winter runtime calculator](/tools/winter-runtime-calculator/) helps with the math; set the battery location to "cold cabin" to see how the cold affects it.
